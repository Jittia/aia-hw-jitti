Moat Momentum

Strengthening moats, found in the filings. Methodology whitepaper v1.1 · 04 November 2026

1. Summary

Moat Momentum screens every US-listed company with SEC XBRL data for signs that its competitive advantage is strengthening: rising returns on capital, pricing power, scale benefits, gains against industry peers and cash-backed profit. Every number comes from audited filings and links to the 10-K it came from.

We tested the method honestly before writing this paper. The screen finds high-quality, improving businesses: three years after selection they still ranked +8.3 points above their industry median on returns, margins and turnover, with clean ROIC 5.4% higher. But it does not yet predict further strengthening, and a portfolio of its picks returned 14.3% a year from 2013 to 2026, about the same as the S&P 500 (14.3%), with more volatility. We report this in full, because a research tool is only as useful as its willingness to show where it fails. Section 11 adds what changed on 3 and 4 November 2026: v1.1 stopped two risk checks from removing healthy large companies, and a quarterly Compounder Signal (v2.0) failed its one-time test on 2020–2023, so it is offered only as an experimental list.

2. The problem

Quality screens find companies that are already great and already priced as such. Analyst moat ratings are subjective, slow to change and cover a limited set of companies. Investors who want to see a moat forming, rather than one that is already recognised, have no transparent, testable, filing-based tool.

3. Academic foundations

  • Value creation: returns on invested capital above the cost of capital, and returns on new capital (Mauboussin & Callahan, “Measuring the Moat”; Koller, Goedhart & Wessels, “Valuation”).
  • Pricing power: gross profitability predicts returns (Novy-Marx, 2013, Journal of Financial Economics).
  • Profit quality: accruals predict earnings declines (Sloan, 1996); simple financial-strength scores (Piotroski, 2000; Mohanram, 2005).
  • Risk: distress (Altman Z, 1968; Z'' variant) and earnings manipulation (Beneish M-Score, 1999).

4. Method

4.1 Data

SEC EDGAR companyfacts and submissions bulk files (retrieved 2026-11-03). Up to 10 fiscal years of 10-K data per company. The store keeps the first value filed for every period and every later restatement, so a past run uses only what was public on its date.

4.2 Clean-up

Operating income is cleaned of one-off items the company tags separately: impairments, restructuring, litigation, acquisition costs and gains on asset sales. Tax is set to one 21% rate for every year so that the 2018 US tax change does not create a false trend. Invested capital = equity + debt + finance leases − cash above 2% of revenue.

4.3 Universe and risk gate

US-listed, revenue at least $100M, at least five fiscal years, not a bank, insurer or REIT. Seven risk checks run before scoring: interest cover, net debt ÷ EBITDA, Altman Z'', Beneish M-Score, dilution, stock-based pay and acquisition-driven goodwill. Each can be off, flag (penalty) or remove.

4.4 Scoring

Thirteen indicators in five themes (value creation 25, pricing power 20, scale 15, pulling away from peers 20, profit quality 20). Each is ranked within its SIC industry on level, five-year trend and consistency, weighted 30/50/20 toward improvement. “Moat Strengthening” requires at least three of five themes improving. 5. Data quality

For 10 companies in 10 industries, three fiscal years each, 331 of 333 non-zero inputs were found printed in the 10-K text, and 60 of 60 hand recalculations matched the engine. The check found two clean-up issues, which were fixed: an operating-income tag that was a segment total, and business-sale gains that sit below operating income.

7. What the evidence supports, and what it does not

  • Supported: the screen identifies companies with above-industry returns on capital and margins that persist for at least three years, from audited data, with every number traceable.
  • Not supported: that a strengthening moat is visible in the numbers before it is recognised, or that the shortlist earns excess returns. Picks tend to be caught near the top of their improvement.

8. Limitations

  • Only one-off items that companies tag separately can be removed.
  • Levels use fiscal-year data. Trailing-twelve-month scoring was tested and not adopted: once the back-test's look-ahead was removed it was no better (shortlist hit rate 34.8% vs 39.0%), and its market return was lower (12.2% vs 15.6% a year).
  • The hurdle rate is 9% for all sectors; EDGAR has no market data for a true cost of capital.
  • Altman Z'' uses book equity and flags some healthy companies with negative equity from buybacks; it is off by default since v1.1.
  • Free price data misses many companies that no longer trade; results are shown with bounds.
  • Banks, insurers, REITs and foreign filers are out of scope in Phase 1.

9. Audit trail and live record

Every official run freezes its shortlist, settings and data date in the public repository with a SHA-256 fingerprint (records of 2026-11-03: v1.0 86cab85d825dbea2…, v1.1 dd5179192bfdd7fb…, experimental v2.0 list c4cf9cf7fa72863e…). The forward record cannot be edited without the change showing.

10. Roadmap

  • Done since v1.0: the v1.1 risk gate and the v2.0 Compounder Signal test (section 11). Next: track both frozen lists every quarter; any new signal is judged only on years after 2023.
  • Phase 2: qualitative layer for the shortlist (transcripts, leadership, business model) with the founder's procurement lens.
  • Phase 3: foreign filers (20-F, IFRS), then Thailand, Hong Kong and Taiwan; optional valuation overlay kept separate from the moat score.

11. What changed since v1.0

Risk gate v1.1 (3 November 2026). Altman Z'' and Beneish M-Score now default to off. In v1.0 they removed 20 of the 100 largest US companies, including NVIDIA, Micron, AbbVie and Oracle, while changing back-test false alarms by less than one point. With v1.1, 1,309 companies pass the gate and 453 are Moat Strengthening. The back-test result is unchanged in substance: shortlist hit rate 26.2% and false alarm 38.8%, against 48.6% and 21.8% for the rest of the universe; market return 15.5% a year from July 2013 to October 2026, against 14.3% for the S&P 500.

Compounder Signal v2.0 (4 November 2026, experimental). The goal is to find companies one to two years before they become moat compounders: within three years, revenue grows at least 20% a year and at least 10 points faster than the industry median, rises every year, clean ROIC stays above 9% in at least two of the three years, gross margin holds and little growth is bought. Ten quarterly signals were fixed in advance; eight passed the training rule on 2013–2019 and were combined with equal weights. The test on 2020–2023, run once, required the top 10% to become compounders at least twice as often as all companies. It reached 1.18×, so it failed. The two methods on the same measure:

v2.0 appears in the screener only as a second, experimental list, next to market value, a clean P/E and filters by industry and size, to point to companies worth a closer look. The price rows are context, not the pass test; the v1.1 list is rebalanced once a year and the v2.0 list every quarter. Both lists are frozen so that results from 2026 onward test them on years neither method has seen.
